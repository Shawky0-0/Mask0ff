## What happened

AFX Trade, a perpetuals exchange on Arbitrum, ran its own bridge. The bridge used
seven validators, with 10,000 voting units shared out unevenly between them, and
required two thirds, 6,667 units, to authorise a transfer.

An attacker obtained the private keys of five validators. Those five held 7,142
units, comfortably above the threshold. They signed one transaction moving
24,150,000 USDC to an address they controlled. The funds went to Ethereum and became
12,467.5 ETH.

Halborn is direct about the route in: "The attack was performed entirely off-chain,
compromising keys via social engineering, malware, or other means."

## Why it worked

Nothing in the contract was wrong. The threshold logic did exactly what it was
written to do. That is the point of this entry.

A two thirds threshold is a statement about independence. It says an attacker must
defeat five separate things. If those five things are five key files on machines the
same operator administers, the attacker defeats one thing. The number seven was
decoration.

The 200 second dispute window is the second half of the story. A dispute window is
only a control if somebody is watching and can act within it. Three and a half
minutes is not a window a human notices, let alone responds inside. It looks like a
control on the architecture diagram and is not one in operation.

## What the fix is, and why the obvious fix would not work

The obvious fix is to raise the threshold, say six of seven. It does not help. If one
compromise reaches five keys it very likely reaches six, because they are the same
kind of key in the same kind of place.

The fix is to make the signers actually independent: different operators, different
organisations, some share held in keys that a human must physically touch. That
costs speed, which is why bridges do not do it, and it is the reason bridges keep
appearing in this folder.

The second fix is to make the dispute window real. Either lengthen it enough that a
human can act, or replace it with an automatic limit, such as a cap on how much can
leave in one transaction.
