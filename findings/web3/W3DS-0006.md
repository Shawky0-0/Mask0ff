## What happened

On 2026-04-14 at about 14:54 UTC, attackers used forged identity documents to convince registrar staff
to hand over control of the domain, then changed the A records to point at their own server. The site
they served was a pixel perfect clone of the real interface, carrying a wallet drainer script.

The drainer did not ask for a private key. It scanned for assets and then generated ERC-2612 permit
signature requests, labelled to the user as a "Security Update" or "Network Verification" step.
Signing granted the attacker broad approval to move the victim's tokens. Reported losses were about
$1.2 million, with one trader losing 219 ETH. The team noticed resolution anomalies within minutes and
still needed roughly 26 hours to get the domain back.

## Why it worked

For a dApp, the front end is not decoration around the security boundary, it is the thing that builds
the transaction the user approves. Whoever controls the served JavaScript controls what the wallet is
asked to sign. So the attacker never needed to touch the contracts, which were fine throughout.

The second half is a user interface problem with a financial consequence. A permit signature is not a
login, it is an authorisation to spend. A wallet prompt that does not clearly state the spender and the
amount lets an attacker relabel that authorisation as a routine maintenance step, and users who have
been trained to click through prompts will click through this one.

## What the fix is, and why the obvious fix would not work

The obvious fix is stronger passwords and multi factor on the registrar account. It would not have
helped here, because the account was never breached. The registrar's own staff moved the domain on the
strength of forged paperwork, so the control has to sit at the registrar's process: a registry lock
that requires manual, out of band confirmation from named people before any change.

The rest is defence in depth that shortens the window: DNSSEC, published build hashes with subresource
integrity so a swapped bundle fails to load, external monitoring that alerts on resolution changes,
and a signing flow that states plainly what is being granted. None of those stop a registrar being
fooled. All of them make the fooling less profitable.
