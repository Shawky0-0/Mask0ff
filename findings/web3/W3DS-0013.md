## What happened

Blockaid tracked five campaigns in April 2026. The pattern was identical each time.

A protocol got hacked. The protocol told its users, correctly, to go and revoke
their token approvals or migrate their assets. Within hours, sometimes the same day,
someone registered a domain that looked like the right place to do that, and pushed
it into the reply threads under the protocol's own announcements.

The domains, defanged: `revokes-drift[.]trade` after Drift, `revoke-kernelsdao[.]com`
after KelpDAO, `revoke-zetachain[.]com` after ZetaChain, `aftermathfi[.]xyz/migrate`
after Aftermath Finance, `exploit-wasabi[.]com/migrate` after Wasabi.

For KelpDAO, Blockaid counted nine coordinated retweets from repurposed accounts
inside six minutes. For ZetaChain, the malicious link was posted in the reply thread
under Blockaid's own security alert. For Wasabi, a fake account impersonating the
protocol replied directly under the official posts.

The user arrives at a site that looks right, connects a wallet expecting to remove
permissions, and signs something that grants permissions instead.

## Why it worked

The attacker did not have to build any of the hard parts. The protocol supplied
them.

The urgency was real and came from the protocol. The instruction to go somewhere and
act was official. The user was already looking for exactly the kind of page the
attacker built. All the attacker added was a domain name and a place to post it,
both of which cost close to nothing and take minutes.

The deeper problem is that "revoke your approvals" is an unfamiliar action. The user
does not have a habit that tells them where it normally happens, so they have no
baseline to compare against. Any plausible page passes.

## What the fix is, and why the obvious fix would not work

The obvious fix is to warn users about phishing. It fails for the same reason it
always fails: the user is being told to distrust links by a message that contains a
link, during the one week they are most motivated to click.

The fix is structural. Do not create a new destination during an incident. Tell
users to log in where they already log in, and put the guidance there. If they must
be sent somewhere, send them to a path on the domain they have used for a year, not
to a fresh name that reads well.

Then do the cheap preparation: work out what the lookalikes would be called and
register them yourself before you need them. It is a small annual cost against a
class of attack that arrives at the worst possible moment.

For Ahmed specifically: this is testable on the fleet with nothing but public WHOIS
and a read of the incident runbook. No target is touched. It is the closest thing in
this folder to work he could do next week.
