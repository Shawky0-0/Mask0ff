## Why this entry is here despite the modest score

CVSS 4.3 is not exciting. Two other things are.

**Bookly is on Ahmed's stack**, and this is the second Bookly bug in this folder after
WPDS-0007. Two independent researchers found two
unrelated defects in the same product within weeks. That is a signal about the product, and
it is worth more than either finding on its own.

**It corroborates this vault's own correction.** WPDS-0007 recorded that every published
source said Bookly was fixed at 27.6, and that reading the source at six tags showed the
sanitisation first appearing at 27.8, with a changelog that never mentioned security. This
advisory independently gives the affected range as "up to and including 27.7", which is the
same boundary from the other direction: 27.8 is where something changed. It does not prove
the earlier correction, because these are two different bugs in two different files, and
saying otherwise would be overreach. But two separate defects both stopping at 27.7 makes
"27.8 was a security release that did not say so" the simpler explanation. **Flagged for
Ahmed, not concluded.**

## What actually happens

Bookly has a mobile app for staff. The app talks to the site through its own small API,
and a staff member signs in to it with a token.

The app asks for an appointment by number. The code takes that number, loads the appointment,
and sends it back.

It never checks whether the appointment is yours.

So a staff member with a valid token can ask for appointment 1, then 2, then 3, and read
every appointment in the business: the customer's name, their contact details, the payment
information, and the internal note that staff write about customers assuming the customer
will never see it and neither will anybody else.

## Why it works

Because signing in and being allowed are two different things, and only the first one was
tested.

The token proved the caller is staff. The code then behaved as though it had proved the
caller is *this* staff member with a claim on *this* record. Nothing in the method connects
the caller to the record at all. The id in the request is the only thing deciding what comes
back, and the person sending the request writes the id.

## How you would test it

Two staff accounts in the lab. Put a canary word in an appointment that belongs to the
second one. Sign in as the first, ask for the second one's appointment by its number, and
look for the canary in the answer.

It reads nothing and writes nothing, which is what makes it a clean test. Run the control
first though: ask for your own appointment, and make sure that works. A negative result from
a broken request is not evidence of a secure system, and that mistake is easy to make.

## The fix, and why the obvious fix would not work

There is no published fix to read, so the invariant field is `___`. What a fix must do is
compare the loaded record's staff id against the caller's, before anything is copied into
the response, and do it for the nested customer appointments too.

The obvious fix is to add that `if` statement to this method. It closes this bug and leaves
the design in place, which means the next method added to the handler will have the same gap
unless whoever writes it remembers. The structural answer is to stop loading by raw id and
scope the query to the caller instead, so that the ownership rule lives in the query and
there is nothing left to forget.

The other tempting fix, making appointment ids unguessable, is worth doing and is not a fix.
It makes enumeration harder without making the access control correct, so the moment an id
leaks by any other route the record is readable again. Hard to guess is not the same as not
allowed.
